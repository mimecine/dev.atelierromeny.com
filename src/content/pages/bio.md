Edlef was born on February 14th, 1926 in Sint-Michielsgestel, a village in the south of the Netherlands, and given a name almost too long: Edlef Wybo Rudolph Arthur ter Haar Romeny. On his canvases it would later shrink to a single word, _Romeny_.

His father, Barend, was a Reformed minister. His mother, Elisabeth, died in 1930, when Edlef was four. He and his brother Barend, a year older, grew up in a series of parsonages, partly in Twente, in the flat, green, east of the country. Somewhere in those quiet rooms he discovered that what he wanted, more than anything, was to make pictures.

By 1943, at seventeen, Edlef was studying stained glass at the art academy in Arnhem under Gerard van Lerven. It is a telling first choice: not paint on canvas but colour held up to the light. It was 1943, in an occupied country, and the world was about to intervene.

## War, and the other side of the world

In 1944 the war reached Arnhem, and Edlef’s studies ended with it. Soon after, like many young Dutchmen after liberation, he volunteered: first alongside British forces, then with the Dutch Marines, who shipped him to the Dutch East Indies.

He went as a Marine, but he appropriately served as a front-line draughtsman, drawing what he saw. It was a strange apprenticeship for a painter, and a hard one. Yet it was in Surabaya, in Java, half a world away from Twente, that Edlef held his first exhibition.

## Antwerp, and light through glass

Back in Europe, a Dutch state scholarship brought him to the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Antwerp, where from 1948 to 1951 he studied with René De Coninck and the academy’s director, the portraitist Isidoor Opsomer. There was a spell in Paris too, at an academy on the rue Notre-Dame-des-Champs, and lessons with the surrealist painter and engraver, Henri Goetz.

The young artist did not wait to graduate before taking on real work. In 1949 he made two large windows for the memorial chapel of Stoottroepen.

Two years later he was given a commission closer to home. His father was now the minister in Heemskerk, and the mayor asked the minister’s son to fill the stairwell of the old town hall with a great stained glass window. It shows a scene from 1418: the people of Kennemerland paying homage to Duke John of Brabant and Jacqueline of Bavaria. A committee that included the director of the Frans Hals Museum approved the design, and on 11 September 1951 it was unveiled, with the local paper hailing a “promising young painter-glazier”. The murals he painted beside it have long since vanished.. The window has had a happier fate: it travelled to the new town hall in 1981 and returned home in 2011, to the stairwell it was made for.

There were exhibitions too, in Amsterdam in 1950 alongside the painter Tonny Ros, and at the university in Utrecht in 1952. Then, in 1955, he left the Netherlands and never resided there again.

## The Swedish years

In 1955 he went north. The same  year he married a Swedish teacher, Ingegerd Öfors, and settled with her in Sweden. Six children followed, for some twenty-five years home was Stockholm and its surroundings.

The light of the north changed his painting. Through the 1960s and 1970s he became a well-known and successful artist in Sweden, working in the spirit of abstract expressionism: big, free fields of colour, including a series of “space compositions” from 1964. He was restless with technique as well. His carborundum etchings were viewed  as the most innovative part of his work, and alongside them came colour etchings, woodcuts, lithographs and enamels. A Dutch newspaper called him,  in 1962 , simply, a colourist with many possibilities.

During those years he showed at Galerie Æsthetica in Stockholm in 1957 and at the Norrköping Art Museum in 1960. He made windows again as well, four small ones for the church at Jonsberg and one for a chapel in Motala. He never let go of the Netherlands, returning again and again to exhibit there.

## Provence

![Edlef and sons in Les Baups](/src/media/assets/PXL_20260930_142451565.MP.webp "Edlef and sons in Les Baups")

There was another light he could not forget. From 1964, perhaps even earlier, he spent his summers in a studio in Provence, enchanted by a landscape unlike anything in Twente or Stockholm. A painting from 1964 already bears the title _Vue de Mon Atelier, Provence_.

When his Swedish marriage ended, he followed that light south. From 1978 he divided his life between Paris and Provence, and in 1980 he married the soprano _Lise Arséguet_. In 1986 he settled in the Luberon for good.

In Provence the abstraction loosened its hold. His biographer called him a colourist and a seeker of beauty, and the paintings of these years bear that out: hill villages, orchards in blossom, flowering trees, the landscape around Villars, all rendered in the warm, saturated colour he had been chasing since Arnhem. Here, too, he found an audience and recognition.

He was a born storyteller. Dutch television came to interview him for Rik Felderhof’s programme _De Stoel_, and Germany’s ZDF had him as a guest. For his eightieth birthday in 2006 he published “_Edlef Romeny (1926), Leven en Werk_”, a book that pairs his own vivid, anecdote-rich memoir with the art historian Roel Smit-Muller’s account of his work.

Edlef ter Haar Romeny died on Christmas Day 2017 in _St. Saturnin-les-Apt_, near Villars, at the age of 91. In 2021 his paintings from Provence went north once more, with an exhibition at _Galleri Flach_ in Stockholm.

## What he left

Few painters range as widely. Over a long working life he created in oil, watercolour, gouache and pastel; in etching, woodcut and lithography; in enamel and collage; in murals and, above all, at the start, in glass. His subjects ran from portraits and still lifes to landscapes and pure abstraction, and back again.

His paintings hang in the Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam and the Västerås Art Museum in Sweden; a self-portrait is in the Kunsthaus Zürich, and his work is also held by the New York Public Library. His windows still stand in the chapel at Beneden-Leeuwen and the old town hall of Heemskerk, and in the churches of Jonsberg and Motala.

The rest is scattered throughout the world where his prints and canvases still appear, carrying that one-word signature: _Romeny_.
