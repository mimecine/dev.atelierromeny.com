He was born on 14 February 1926 in Sint-Michielsgestel, a village in the Catholic south of the Netherlands, and given a name almost too long for a small boy to carry: _Edlef Wybo Rudolph Arthur ter Haar Romeny_. On his canvases it would later shrink to a single word, _Romeny_.

His father, Barend, was a Reformed minister. His mother, Elisabeth, died in 1930, when Edlef was four. He and his brother Barend, a year older, grew up in a series of parsonages, partly in Twente, in the flat green east of the country. Somewhere in those quiet Protestant rooms the boy discovered that what he wanted, more than anything, was to make pictures.

By 1943, at seventeen, he was studying stained glass at the art academy in Arnhem under Gerard van Lerven. It is a telling first choice: not paint on canvas but colour held up to the light. It was also 1943, in an occupied country, and the world was about to intervene.

## War, and the other side of the world

In 1944 the war reached Arnhem, and his studies ended with it. Like many young Dutchmen after liberation, he volunteered: first alongside British forces, then with the Dutch Marines, who shipped him to the Dutch East Indies as the colony fought for its independence.

He went as a marine, but he served as a front-line draughtsman, drawing what he saw during the so-called police actions. It was a strange apprenticeship for a painter, and a hard one. Yet it was in Surabaya, on Java, half a world from Twente, that Edlef ter Haar Romeny held his very first exhibition.

## Antwerp, and light through glass

Back in Europe, a Dutch state scholarship took him to the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Antwerp, where from 1948 to 1951 he learned from René De Coninck and from the academy's director, the portraitist Isidoor Opsomer. There was a spell in Paris too, at an academy on the rue Notre-Dame-des-Champs, and lessons with Henri Goetz.

The young artist did not wait to graduate before taking on real work. In 1949 he made two large windows for the memorial chapel of the Stoottroepen, the regiment that had fought to free the river land around Beneden-Leeuwen. Every October, veterans and villagers still gather there beneath his glass.

Two years later came a commission closer to home. His father was now the minister in Heemskerk, and the mayor asked the minister's son to fill the stairwell of the old town hall with a great painted window. It shows a scene from 1418: the people of Kennemerland paying homage to Duke John of Brabant and Jacqueline of Bavaria. A committee that included the director of the Frans Hals Museum approved the design, and on 11 September 1951 it was unveiled, with the local paper hailing a "promising young painter-glazier". The murals he painted beside it have long since vanished under new paint. The window has had a happier fate: it travelled to the new town hall in 1981 and came home again in 2011, to the stairwell it was made for.

There were exhibitions too, in Amsterdam in 1950 alongside the painter Tonny Ros, and at the university in Utrecht in 1952. Then, in 1955, he left the Netherlands, and never lived there again.

## The Swedish years

In 1955 he went north. That year he married a Swedish teacher, Ingegerd Öfors, and settled with her in Sweden. Six children followed, among them Odorico, who would become a painter himself. For some twenty-five years home was Stockholm and its surroundings.

The light of the north changed his painting. Through the 1960s and 1970s he became a well-known and successful artist in Sweden, working in the spirit of abstract expressionism: big, free fields of colour, including a series of "space compositions" from 1964. He was restless with technique, too. His carborundum etchings, built up from grit on the plate, were seen as the most innovative part of his work, and alongside them came colour etchings, woodcuts, lithographs and enamels. A Dutch newspaper in 1962 called him, simply, a colourist with many possibilities.

He showed at Galerie Æsthetica in Stockholm in 1957 and at the Norrköping Art Museum in 1960. He made windows again as well, four small ones for the church at Jonsberg and one for a chapel in Motala. And he never let go of the Netherlands, returning again and again to exhibit there. Later, the 1986 murder of Olof Palme, the Swedish prime minister, moved him to paint an entire series in response.

## Provence

But there was another light he could not forget. From 1965, perhaps even earlier, he spent his summers in a studio in Provence, enchanted by a landscape unlike anything in Twente or Stockholm. A painting from 1964 already bears the title _Vue de mon Abelier, Provence_.

When his Swedish marriage ended, he followed that light south. From 1978 he divided his life between Paris and Provence, and in 1980 he married his first love from his youth, the soprano Lise Arséguet. In 1986 he settled in the Luberon for good.

In Provence the abstraction loosened its hold. His biographer called him a colourist and a seeker of beauty, and the paintings of these years bear that out: hill villages, orchards in blossom, flowering trees, the landscape around Villars, all rendered in the warm, saturated colour he had been chasing since Arnhem. Here, too, he found an audience and recognition.

He was a born storyteller. Dutch television came to interview him for Rik Felderhof's programme _De Stoel_, and Germany's ZDF had him as a guest. For his eightieth birthday in 2006 he published _Edlef Romeny (1926), leven en werk_, a book that pairs his own vivid, anecdote-rich memoir with the art historian Roel Smit-Muller's account of his work.

Edlef ter Haar Romeny died on Christmas Day 2017 at Les Baups, near Villars, at the age of 91. In 2021 his _Paintings from Provence_ went north once more, to Galleri Flach in Stockholm.

## What he left

Few painters range as widely. Over a long working life he worked in oil, watercolour, gouache and pastel; in etching, woodcut and lithography; in enamel and collage; in murals and, above all at the start, in glass. His subjects ran from portraits and still lifes to landscapes and pure abstraction, and back again.

His paintings hang in the Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam and the Västerås Art Museum in Sweden. A self-portrait is in the Kunsthaus Zürich, and his work is also held by the New York Public Library. His windows still stand in the chapel at Beneden-Leeuwen and the old town hall of Heemskerk, and in the churches of Jonsberg and Motala.

The rest is scattered across the homes of Sweden, the Netherlands and France, where his prints and canvases still turn up, carrying that one-word signature: _Romeny_.
